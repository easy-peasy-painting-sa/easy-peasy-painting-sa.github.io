Cabinet Painting Terms, Decoded for San Antonio Homeowners

Painters have their own vocabulary, and an estimate visit can turn into a blur of words like "enamel" and "cure" that nobody stops to define. Here are the terms worth knowing, grouped by when you are likely to hear them. Skim it once and keep it handy.

Parts of the cabinet

Box (or carcass). The fixed frame attached to the wall. If the boxes are sound, the cabinets are good candidates for paint.

Face frame. The strip of wood around the front of the box that the doors close against. It gets painted in place.

Door and drawer front. The removable pieces. These take the most wear and get the most attention.

Shaker, raised panel, slab. Door styles. Shaker has a flat center with a square border, raised panel has a contoured center, and slab is completely flat. More profile means more edges to sand and coat.

Thermofoil. A vinyl skin heat-wrapped over fiberboard, common in builder-grade kitchens around San Antonio. It behaves differently from wood, so mention it up front if you suspect you have it.

Prep words

Degreasing. Washing every surface with a cleaner that cuts cooking residue. Paint will not stick to grease, even grease you cannot see.

Scuff sanding. A light sanding that dulls the old finish so primer has something to grip. It does not mean stripping to bare wood.

Filling. Patching dings, worn edges and old hardware holes before primer.

Masking. Covering counters, floors, appliances and openings with paper and plastic. Good masking is why the rest of your kitchen stays clean.

Bonding primer. A primer made to grab slick surfaces such as old varnish or factory finishes. On cabinets it is the layer that everything else depends on.

Bleed-through. Tannins or old stain ghosting up through light paint, often on oak and knotty woods. The right primer blocks it.

Finish words

Enamel. A paint that dries to a hard shell instead of the softer film used on walls. It is what lets a cabinet door survive fingernails and scrubbing.

Sprayed finish. Paint applied with a spray gun for a smooth surface with no brush marks.

Fine-finish application. Careful brush and roller work using tools designed to level out. Often used on face frames.

Sheen. How shiny the dried paint is. Satin and semi-gloss are the usual cabinet picks because they wipe clean.

Thin coats. Several light layers instead of one heavy one. Heavy coats sag and stay soft.

Dry time vs. cure time. Dry means you can touch it. Cured means it has reached full hardness, which takes longer. Be gentle with doors during that window.

Grain show-through. Open-grained woods like oak keep some texture under paint. Ask whether the grain will be filled or left visible so the result is not a surprise.

Words on the estimate

Scope. Exactly what is being painted: which rooms, fronts only or both sides of the doors, interiors or not.

Color match. Mixing paint to a specific manufacturer color. Easy Peasy Painting Company states that it matches any Sherwin-Williams color and will help you choose.

Timeline. The number of working days. Most kitchens run several days because prep and coats cannot be rushed.

Walkthrough. The last step, where you and the crew look over the finished kitchen together and any touch-ups get handled.

Touch-up. A small repair to a nick or missed spot.

A local note

You will hear "humidity" a lot in San Antonio, and for good reason. Damp Gulf air slows drying, so a painter who builds extra time between coats here is being careful, not slow.
